01 // CONTEXT

Context: Mission & Need

Supporting high-intake rehabilitation at Western Pennsylvania's premier wildlife sanctuary.

The Humane Animal Rescue of Pittsburgh (HARP) Wildlife Center rehabilitates over 4,000 injured, orphaned, and displaced wild animals annually across Western Pennsylvania. During peak fall and winter admissions, the center experiences severe capacity bottlenecks for both small mammal conditioning and specialized reptilian housing.

To support their medical release pipeline, HARP required two distinct architectural deliverables built to stringent wildlife rehabilitation specifications:

10 Oversized Mammal Shelters

High-durability nesting boxes designed for rehabilitating squirrels, opossums, and small woodland mammals. Boxes must withstand extreme winter freeze-thaw cycles while preventing moisture ingress and claw wear.

1 Custom 2-Turtle Habitat Enclosure

A dedicated, reinforced indoor/outdoor rehabilitation habitat sized for two recovering native turtles. Required full ventilation, natural light penetration via heavy-gauge wire, and a secure access hatch preventing escape and predator intrusion.

Project Objective: Act as the sole candidate engineer and project manager: synthesize beneficiary blueprints into precise bill-of-materials (BOM), negotiate supplier discounts, pilot-test fabrication, and coordinate an 11-person team across 165 hours with zero defect handovers.
02 // APPROACH

Approach: Material Decisions

Adapting specifications when commercial supply chains fail to meet raw requirements.

1. The Cedar-to-Poplar Material Tradeoff

The initial engineering proposal called for naturally rot-resistant 1-inch thick cedar lumber for the 10 mammal nesting boxes. Upon auditing regional supplier inventories, no local lumber yard or Home Depot carried 1-inch untreated cedar stock or pre-treated dimensioned lumber safe for direct animal contact (chemical pressure-treating compounds like CCA or ACQ leach toxins harmful to small mammals).

Engineering Resolution

Adapted the material bill to 1-inch dense poplar wood, engineered with an external non-toxic, pet-safe sealant cured post-assembly. Poplar offered superior structural stability and splinter resistance over pine, while the post-fabrication treatment created a hydrophobic barrier against rain and snow without risking chemical ingestion by animals.

2. 1:1 Prototype Testing Before Batch Manufacturing

Rather than distributing cut lists and having 11 volunteers cut lumber simultaneously, Dhruv built a single 1:1 prototype animal box in advance. This pilot build uncovered several critical physical constraints:

  • Kerf Compensation: Saw blade thickness was shifting internal box dimensions by 1/8", preventing the floor plate from seating flush into the grooved side panels.
  • Pre-drilling Pilot Holes: 1-inch poplar was prone to splitting near end-grain joints when driven with 2.5" exterior wood screws without prior countersinking.
  • Angle Jigs: Sloped drainage roofs required exact 15-degree miter cuts that were difficult to replicate freehand on job site circular saws.

By identifying these flaws on Unit #1, custom wooden drilling jigs and angle stops were fabricated prior to volunteer arrival, eliminating material scrap across the remaining 9 units.

03 // PROTOTYPING

Hardware Iterations

Engineering the turtle enclosure access door for dual-action security.

The single most mechanically challenging component of the entire project was the access hatch for the two-turtle habitat enclosure. The door had to satisfy two competing operational constraints:

  1. Predator & Escape Defense: Must seal with zero flex or edge gaps, preventing raccoons, foxes, or rodents from prying open the wire mesh while preventing turtles from wedging shells beneath the frame.
  2. Ergonomic Staff Access: Must be easily operable with one gloved hand by HARP rehabilitation veterinarians carrying feeding trays or medical supplies.
3 Hinge Geometry Trials

Initial light-duty strap hinges sagged under the weight of the reinforced wire-mesh frame, causing the latch to misalign by 4mm. Re-engineered with heavy-gauge galvanized T-hinges spaced across reinforced vertical studs to maintain square alignment under continuous outdoor cycling.

3 Latch Sizing Experiments

Standard barrel bolts proved too loose against vibration, while heavy slide-bolts caused binding against moisture-swollen lumber. The final configuration utilized a self-adjusting zinc-plated safety hasp with a dual-spring retention pin, tolerating up to 3mm of seasonal wood expansion without jamming.

04 // SUPPLY CHAIN

Supplier Negotiation

Securing $800+ in corporate commercial subsidies without third-party fundraising.

BSA service projects traditionally rely on public fundraising campaigns (car washes, bake sales, donor letters). To maximize efficiency and reduce administrative overhead, Dhruv chose an alternative strategy: direct corporate commercial sponsorship through supplier negotiation.

The Pitch to Home Depot Leadership

Dhruv drafted comprehensive, professional material takeoff sheets directly derived from the HARP blueprints. Armed with official BSA project authorization letters and HARP beneficiary endorsements, he met in person with store managers across two regional Home Depot locations.

Store #1 Negotiation (Turtle Habitat) Secured 70% corporate lumber discount + $100 off hardware, chicken wire, and structural fasteners.
Store #2 Negotiation (Animal Shelters) Pitched local store near the HARP facility; secured an identical 70% commercial discount on all 1-inch poplar lumber.
Total Financial Impact Reduced commercial retail value from ~$1,200+ down to $380.30 total out-of-pocket outlay ($0 public debt).

Following project completion, Dhruv returned in person to the store managers with an executive presentation featuring photographs of the deployed shelters and turtle habitat at HARP to close the sponsorship loop and maintain institutional goodwill for future community scouts.

05 // OPERATIONS

Labor Pipelining

Eliminating idle volunteer concentration through manufacturing station architecture.

The Volunteer Idle Bottleneck & Restructuring

In his post-project debrief, Dhruv identified the primary operational challenge of managing an 11-person volunteer crew:

"The most difficult aspect was optimizing the volunteers for the animal boxes. At times, it felt as though some volunteers were doing absolutely nothing while others were doing nearly all the work. This concentration of work needed to be better spread."

When volunteers congregated around a single workbench, only two people could drive screws or hold boards while nine stood idle. To eliminate this assembly bottleneck, Dhruv re-architected the job site into a 5-station parallel manufacturing pipeline:

Station 1 // Dimensioning & Cut Off

Adult leaders operating miter saws cutting boards strictly to pre-calculated cut sheets, delivering dimensional lumber to staging tables.

Station 2 // Jigs & Pilot Drilling

Scouts using custom wooden drill guides to pre-drill pilot holes and countersinks at exact 2" intervals, eliminating wood splitting.

Station 3 // Sub-Assembly & Squaring

Two paired teams fastening 4-sided box frames with clamping squares, ensuring 90-degree internal joints.

Station 4 // Mesh Tensioning & Hardware

Cutting heavy-gauge chicken wire with aviation snips, stretching across frames with pneumatic staplers, and installing hinges.

Station 5 // Quality Inspection & Sealing

Sanding sharp edges, testing latch operation, and applying non-toxic weatherproofing sealant.

07 // RETROSPECTIVE

What I would change

Reflections on procurement lead times and template prefabrication.

If executing this service project again, I would finalize commercial supplier accounts and bulk lumber discount agreements earlier in the scheduling phase to prevent raw material lead-time bottlenecks. Furthermore, pre-cutting standardized dimensional templates and pilot holes in an offsite workshop would have allowed volunteer crews to focus purely on rapid assembly, reducing total on-site labor hours while maintaining tight construction tolerances.
